Why a video won't fit a size limit (and what the numbers look like)

File size is bitrate times duration. Once you see the arithmetic, it is clear why a short clip fits easily and a long one can't.

The one equation

A video file's size is roughly its total bitrate multiplied by its length. To fit a limit, the bitrate has to come down as the clip gets longer. Quality depends on how many bits you can spend per second.

An example at 10 MB

FitClip reserves a share of the limit for the container and rate-control overshoot, and sets aside 96 kbps for audio. Using a 7% reserve, here is the rough arithmetic for a 10 MB limit (10,000,000 bytes × 0.93 × 8 bits ÷ seconds, minus 96 kbps for audio). These are illustrations of the maths, not a promise of what the app will produce for your clip.

Clip lengthTotal budgetLeft for video
30 secondsabout 2.48 Mbpsabout 2.38 Mbps
1 minuteabout 1.24 Mbpsabout 1.14 Mbps
2 minutesabout 0.62 Mbpsabout 0.52 Mbps
5 minutesabout 0.25 Mbpsabout 0.15 Mbps

Each doubling of length halves what is left for the picture. By five minutes the video has only about 150 kbps to work with.

Where FitClip says no

If the video budget falls below about 150 kbps, FitClip reports “too long for this size” rather than delivering a blurry result. That is a deliberate choice: an honest message is more useful than a file that technically fits but can't be watched.

What you can do

  • Choose a larger limit, if the destination allows it.
  • Use a shorter clip. FitClip doesn't trim, so cut the clip in another tool first.
  • Share a link instead of the file, if the service allows it.

FitClip also picks the output resolution so there are enough bits per pixel to hold the picture together: a lower resolution at a tight limit looks better than full resolution with heavy blocking.
